# How PEDRs actually work, and why everyone hates them

Research notes behind the design of this tool. Assembled September 2026 from
RIBA and ARB guidance, university PSA handbooks, and accounts written by people
who have been through it. **Verify anything you are going to rely on** — the
authoritative source is the RIBA PEDR system at pedr.co.uk and your own PSA.
Sources are listed at the bottom.

---

## 1. The shape of the thing

**PEDR** is the Professional Experience and Development Record: RIBA's system
for recording the practical experience you need before you can sit Part 3 and
register as an architect. It lives at pedr.co.uk.

The unit is a **quarter**. One record sheet covers up to three consecutive
months of experience. You need **24 months** of practical experience, so you
need **8 completed sheets**.

Three people touch every sheet, in a fixed order:

| Who | Where they sit | What they do |
|---|---|---|
| **You** (the candidate) | In practice | Write the sheet |
| **Employment Mentor** | In your practice, supervises you directly | Discusses the period with you, comments, signs |
| **PSA** (Professional Studies Advisor) | At a school of architecture | Reviews breadth, scope and adequacy of experience; comments; gives final approval |

The mentor has "detailed professional knowledge of your work" and does not have
to be the person who employs you. The PSA is the quality gate — they are
assessing whether your experience is broad enough, not just whether you turned
up.

### What is on a sheet

The live sheet is organised as:

1. **General Information** — employer, dates, hours, category and location of
   experience, supervisor details
2. **Describe Projects** — a description of each project you worked on
3. **Record Activities** — the tasks you did, with **hours recorded against RIBA
   Plan of Work stages**, plus a separate **Office Management** section
4. **Reflect on Experience** — a five-box reflective structure, redrafted in
   recent revisions specifically "to encourage further reflection and
   self-criticism"
5. **Student / Employment Mentor Appraisal** — where you record the outcome of
   the discussion with your mentor
6. **PSA comments** — structured feedback at the end of the period

**Office Management** is a first-class section and people forget it exists. It
covers marketing, practice strategy, attending Part 3 course lectures, CPD, IT,
office training, mentoring and holiday absence. It is where a large amount of
genuinely creditable PC4 experience hides.

### Categories and locations

Every period of experience is classified. Categories were formerly labelled A–K
and are now **i / ii / iii**:

- **Category i** — practice in the UK, EEA, Channel Islands or Isle of Man,
  under the *direct supervision* of an architect registered with ARB or
  registered in that territory
- **Category ii** — practice anywhere else in the world, under the direct
  supervision of an architect registered in that territory
- **Category iii** — other relevant construction-industry experience, not under
  the direct supervision of a registered architect. Accepted only in limited
  amounts

"Direct supervision" means the supervisor has responsibility for and control
over the work. Location is recorded as UK / EEA-Channel Islands-Isle of Man /
Overseas.

### The rules that decide whether you can sit

- Minimum **24 months** total practical experience
- At least **12 of those 24 months** must fall in the **two years immediately
  before** the Part 3 exam

The second rule is the one that catches people. Experience logged years ago and
then abandoned stops counting toward the recency requirement, and no amount of
old sheets fixes it.

## 2. The deadlines nobody tells you about clearly

This is the single most actionable thing in the research, and it is buried in
PDFs.

- A sheet covers a **maximum of 3 months**
- A sheet must be completed **no later than 2 months after the end of the
  period it covers**
- Your PSA aims to review, comment and sign **within 1 month of submission**

Miss the two-month window and the sheet is **late**. In practice that means:

1. The PSA cannot meaningfully comment on your experience or give feedback —
   the point of the exercise is lost
2. A pattern of late sheets "might reflect badly on your time management
   skills" when you sit Part 3

So each sheet has a real due date — *period end + 2 months* — and the number of
late sheets is a number an examiner can count. **No tool I could find surfaces
this.** That is a product.

Separately: universities are slow. Multiple accounts describe waiting **three
months or more** to get sheets back, and a "fairly strong consensus" that you
will spend time chasing. So the clock you care about is not one clock, it is
three: your writing deadline, the mentor's turnaround, and the PSA's turnaround.

A technical wrinkle worth knowing: the PEDR site treats two periods of
experience at the same workplace with **less than 7 days between them** as one
continuous experience. Gaps you record can silently merge.

## 3. Why people find it painful

Assembled from first-hand accounts. In rough order of how much damage each does.

### 3.1 It is written from memory, months late
The dominant failure. You sit down at the end of a quarter — or, more often,
two months after the end of a quarter — and try to reconstruct thirteen weeks.
Accounts describe PEDRs as "such an exhausting document to write" with
"basically everyone" complaining about them.

The universal advice from people who got through it comfortably is the same:
**keep a daily diary or spreadsheet**. A few minutes a day. Tasks, hours,
activities. Then writing the sheet becomes transcription instead of archaeology.

Almost nobody does this, because the tool for it does not exist and a
spreadsheet on a laptop is not where you are when the thing happens.

### 3.2 People describe instead of reflect
The most-cited weakness in the writing itself. "A common mistake in reflective
writing generally is that people focus too much on describing their experience
rather than reflecting on it." Guidance is consistent: **write in the first
person**, focus on your thoughts and judgements, not a narration of events.

The five reflective boxes were deliberately reworded to push self-criticism.
A quarter where everything went smoothly gives you nothing to write in them —
which is why capturing friction *as it happens* matters more than capturing
achievements.

### 3.3 Length and prose quality
Examiners are "very much of the opinion that less is more", with a target of
around **6 pages** per log sheet. And: "do not let bad grammar, poor spelling
and rambling prose stand in the way of the Examiners quickly and clearly
understanding your experience."

So both failure modes are real — a wall of undifferentiated text, and a sheet
so thin there is nothing to assess. Bullet points work for project tasks;
prose works for reflection.

### 3.4 Gaps discovered far too late
The PSA is assessing **breadth**. In a large practice with specialised teams
you can log two perfect years and have nothing against Stage 5, Stage 6 or PC5,
because you have never been near a site or a building contract.

Found at month 22, that is a crisis. Found at month 6, it is a conversation with
your team leader about being put on a job that is on site.

### 3.5 Chasing signatures
Two humans have to act before a sheet is finished, and both have day jobs.
Nothing in the official system nags them on your behalf.

### 3.6 Hours against stages is guesswork
The sheet wants hours attributed to RIBA stages. Reconstructed from memory,
these numbers are fiction, and everyone knows they are fiction. If your practice
runs timesheets, that data already exists and is contemporaneous.

### 3.7 The reasons sheets actually get marked down
Recurring: poor link between the submission and the PEDRs; missing PEDR
overview; little reflection in the overview; little critical professional view
of practice management.

## 4. What the record has to prove by the end

At the end of the qualifying period there is a **Reflective Experience Summary
(RES)**, and the Part 3 submission as a whole is three things: a professional
**CV**, the **PEDR**, and a **career appraisal**.

The RES has to demonstrate:

- **Breadth** across RIBA Plan of Work stages
- **Development over time** — that you were doing harder things in year two
  than in year one
- **Specific examples** against the professional criteria

"Development over time" is the requirement most invisible during logging. You
cannot show a trajectory you did not record, and it cannot be reconstructed
from memory two years later.

## 5. What this means for the product

Mapping each pain point to a decision in this codebase.

| Pain | What we build |
|---|---|
| Written from memory months late | **Dump** — a phone-first box you can open and empty your head into, day or week at a time. Parsed into structured records automatically. This is the diary everyone is told to keep and nobody keeps |
| Deadline drift, late sheets | A **deadline engine**: every quarter gets a due date at period end + 2 months, days remaining, and a count of late sheets. Reminders that land in your phone calendar |
| Description instead of reflection | Scoring that weights **friction and reflection above output**, and prompts that ask what went wrong rather than what got done |
| Length and prose | Word budgets per section against the "around 6 pages" target, and generated drafts written in the first person |
| Gaps found too late | **Coverage**: stages 0–7 and PC1–PC5, with early warning while there is still time to act |
| Chasing signatures | Track the sign-off chain with the age of each stage, so "your mentor has had this 24 days" is visible |
| Hours by stage is guesswork | **Timesheet import**. If your practice logs hours by project and phase, that is contemporaneous evidence sitting in a system |
| Development over time not shown | Score and coverage **trends** across quarters, so the trajectory is in the data before you have to write about it |
| Office Management forgotten | A first-class category, not an afterthought |
| No idea what any of this means | A **guidance layer** written for someone on day one |

### Design consequences that follow

1. **The record is the entries, not the sheet.** Sheets are generated from the
   underlying record. That means a sheet can be regenerated, a period can be
   re-cut, and nothing has to be retyped.
2. **Never invent hours.** A mentor signs these. An estimated number must be
   visibly estimated.
3. **Capture friction at the point it happens.** The reflective boxes are
   graded on it, and nobody remembers a Tuesday annoyance three months later.
4. **Every derived claim shows its working.** If we say a week scored 60, the
   breakdown says why, and if we tag an entry Stage 5, you can see the words
   that made us think so.
5. **Guidance is in the product, not in a PDF.** The people who need it most
   are the ones who have not read the PDF.
